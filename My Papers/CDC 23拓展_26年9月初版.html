Abstract

Finite communication deadlines make Byzantine-resilient sensing a problem of both corrupted reports and selectively delivered evidence. We study binary state estimation at a trusted sink that receives authenticated source tokens over a known network. Compromised nodes can falsify their own reports and filter forwarded tokens according to a fixed state-aware mechanism. We distinguish four observation levels that retain counts, source identities, arrival times, or their joint record. We propose witness-path erasure fusion, which uses a shortest-path tree to organize evidence into disjoint source groups. Missing or late evidence certifies that its witness group contains a compromised node; the detector erases flagged groups and applies majority voting to the remainder. We derive a residual attack-budget certificate and a conditional error bound without treating all retained evidence as unbiased. For networks having unique paths within the deadline, we obtain an exact single-attacker risk formula. A two-round ring specialization attains the clean-constrained minimax value with a deterministic rule and strictly separates its performance from every count-based rule over an explicit accuracy interval. The detector requires linear online work and no attack-distribution training. Exact-arithmetic checks support the structural identities; broader performance validation remains to be completed. The results identify conditions under which provenance enables inexpensive, certifiable defense rather than merely increasing the observation dimension.

Index Terms— Byzantine resilience, distributed detection, finite-horizon inference, information hierarchy, relay censoring, source provenance.

I. Introduction

Networked sensing combines individually unreliable observations to decide a common environmental state. The security objective is not necessarily to recover every message or identify every malicious node. It is to preserve the reliability of the collective decision when part of the communication infrastructure is adversarial. Our earlier ring-lattice study formulated the interaction between an operator and a Byzantine attacker through a consensus-threshold game [1]. The present work addresses a different operational constraint: a trusted sink must decide after a finite number of local communication rounds, and it cannot read all original observations directly.

A representative setting is a perimeter-monitoring network whose local sensors report a binary abnormal-condition indicator to a protected gateway. This setting motivates a deadline and a trusted decision endpoint; it is not presented as an existing deployment of the proposed algorithm. Selective forwarding is a recognized threat in low-power multihop networks [2]. A compromised relay may pass some source reports and suppress others. If that selection depends on the label, received evidence is not a label-independent subsample of the original observations. Network structure then affects not only the quantity of evidence, but also which statistical information reaches the sink before the deadline.

The available information is equally important. A counting detector sees aggregate votes. An identity-aware detector can associate a missing or received vote with its source. A time-aware detector can distinguish arrivals across rounds without necessarily knowing the source, while a joint record preserves both associations. These representations restrict the defense rules that can be implemented. Their set-inclusion relationships alone do not establish a useful security result: a richer representation always permits the detector to ignore information. The substantive questions are when the additional record supports a better worst-case decision, and whether a simple rule can realize that advantage.

A. Related Work and the Specific Gap

Consensus-based Byzantine detection has studied attacks on networked statistics and robust weighted updates [3]. Its iterative state dynamics and local decisions differ from the fixed source tokens and single terminal decision considered here. Detection in tree networks has also been analyzed through attack optimization, detector design, and reputation mechanisms [4]. Consequently, neither structured topology nor Byzantine binary detection is itself a new problem. Our focus is the joint role of source attribution and delivery time under a fixed decision deadline.

Audit-bit schemes use additional consistency information to improve distributed Bayesian detection. Quan et al. [5] analyze strategic attacks on traditional audit-bit processing and develop enhanced fusion schemes. Their paired direct and relayed reports are an important related example of auxiliary information supporting defense. Our detector instead uses the source records and locally measured arrival times already generated by the specified flooding protocol. It does not add direct report channels or audit transmissions. The distinction must be made at the observation-model and guarantee levels, not merely through the name of the algorithm.

Trust-assisted hypothesis testing supplies another close comparison. Cavorsi et al. [6] develop detectors using stochastic trust observations in addition to reported measurements. Here no separate trust-sensing channel is assumed. A group-level certificate follows from a deterministic shortest-path implication. Disjoint-path ideas also have a long history in Byzantine-resilient topology discovery and message delivery [7]. We assume a known topology and do not claim to solve either task. A witness path is only an analytical route that would ensure timely delivery if its nodes were loyal; the actual message path need not be observed.

These distinctions lead to the central question of this paper: can delivery provenance certify enough of an adversarial effect to support a low-cost detector, without estimating a least-favorable attack distribution or learning per-source trust weights? A certificate must do more than flag unusual data. Its probabilistic use must remain valid even though the decision to retain evidence depends on the attack and the observations. Otherwise, the detector would reintroduce the selection bias that motivated the problem.

B. Approach and Contributions

We introduce witness-path erasure fusion (WPEF). A shortest-path tree partitions the expected sources into root branches. A missing or late token certifies the presence of at least one compromised node in its branch. WPEF erases flagged branches and votes on the remainder, with a deterministic sink-following tie rule. The method leaves the communication protocol unchanged.

The first contribution is a general-graph connection between delivery records, residual attack budget, and conditional decision risk. The proof identifies a fixed set of entirely loyal witness groups before conditioning on the observed erasures. This avoids the incorrect assumption that every retained observation is an unbiased sensor sample.

The second contribution is a structural performance characterization. We derive an exact single-attacker risk expression for networks with unique source-to-sink paths within the deadline. Its ring specialization yields a deterministic minimax detector in a two-round regime and a strict comparison against all count-based rules on an explicit parameter interval. The comparison uses a count-class lower bound and an attainable provenance-based upper bound, rather than a fixed-mixture posterior argument alone.

The third contribution is a constructive implementation and cost analysis. WPEF requires no attack-distribution calibration. Its additional online work is linear in the number of received source records and witness groups. We distinguish dynamic counters, static topology maps, and the pre-existing authentication and duplicate-suppression costs. Independent exact checks examine the certificates and formulas on rings, trees, and graphs with alternative routes.

This paper extends [1] through a different observation architecture, a finite deadline, an information hierarchy, and a new erasure detector. It does not transfer the earlier threshold-game equilibrium to the present single-sink problem. Section II specifies the model and comparison criterion. Section III describes WPEF. Section IV proves the general certificate and risk bound. Section V studies structured networks and information separation. Section VI reports the available exact verification. Section VII discusses the scope of the results, and Section VIII concludes.

III. Witness-Path Erasure Fusion

A. Witness Groups and the Erasure Decision

Fix a breadth-first-search tree 𝒯\mathcal T rooted at ff, with ties resolved by a predetermined node ordering. A breadth-first-search tree supplies a shortest path from every vertex to the root [8]. Retain its vertices in BTB_T. Let qq be the number of root children, let [q]={1,…,q}[q]=\{1,\ldots,q\}, and let PgP_g be the expected external vertices in child gg’s subtree. Then

BT\{f}=⨆g∈[q]Pg,sg=|Pg|. B_T\setminus\{f\}=\bigsqcup_{g\in[q]}P_g, \qquad s_g=|P_g|. \tag{8}

Here sgs_g is the size of witness group gg. For any source j∈Pgj\in P_g, its tree path to ff has length djd_j and stays within Pg∪{f}P_g\cup\{f\}. The groups can be branching subtrees rather than single chains. Cross-edges remain available to the actual flooding process. Thus the witness tree is not a routing restriction.

WPEF flags a group if any expected source in it is absent or later than its clean shortest arrival:

Ag=𝟏{∃j∈Pg:τj>Torτj>dj},ℰ={g∈[q]:Ag=1}. A_g=\mathbf 1\{\exists j\in P_g:\tau_j>T\ \text{or}\ \tau_j>d_j\}, \qquad \mathcal E=\{g\in[q]:A_g=1\}. \tag{9}

The symbol 𝟏{⋅}\mathbf 1\{\cdot\} is the indicator of its condition, and ℰ\mathcal E is the random set of erased groups. A missing record has τj=∞\tau_j=\infty, so the two conditions are intentionally compatible. All sources in an unflagged group have arrived. WPEF forms

SW=sf+∑g∉ℰ∑j∈PgLj,ϕW={+1,SW>0,−1,SW<0,sf,SW=0. S_{\mathrm W}=s_f+\sum_{g\notin\mathcal E}\sum_{j\in P_g}L_j, \qquad \phi_{\mathrm W}= \begin{cases} +1,&S_{\mathrm W}>0,\\ -1,&S_{\mathrm W}<0,\\ s_f,&S_{\mathrm W}=0. \end{cases} \tag{10}

This rule uses Full information through source–time association, but compresses it to group anomalies and vote sums. It is not asserted to be a sufficient statistic for Full inference. An unflagged group is not certified loyal; a flagged group is not declared entirely malicious. Erasing a whole group intentionally sacrifices some potentially useful observations in return for a tractable structural guarantee.

B. Streaming Implementation

For each source jj, precompute its group index g(j)g(j) and normal distance djd_j. For each group, maintain a received count rgr_g, a signed sum zgz_g, and a Boolean late flag bgb_g. These three quantities are initialized to zero, zero, and false, respectively. They are internal counters, not additional sensor measurements.

Algorithm 1. Witness-path erasure fusion

Input: trusted sink bit sf; horizon T; static maps group[j], dist[j];
       expected group sizes size[g]; first accepted external-source records.
Output: one binary decision at round T.

Topology preprocessing:
    Build a fixed breadth-first-search tree rooted at the sink.
    Keep vertices with distance at most T.
    Assign each external vertex to its root-child subtree.
    Store group[j], dist[j], and size[g].

At the start of an episode:
    For each group g:
        received[g] = 0; vote_sum[g] = 0; late[g] = false.

For each first accepted expected source record (j, label, arrival_round):
    g = group[j].
    received[g] += 1.
    vote_sum[g] += label.
    late[g] = late[g] OR (arrival_round > dist[j]).

At the deadline:
    score = sf.
    For each group g:
        erased = (received[g] < size[g]) OR late[g].
        If not erased: score += vote_sum[g].
    If score > 0: return +1.
    If score < 0: return -1.
    Return sf.

Authentication and duplicate suppression are supplied by the unchanged protocol. No source contributes twice. Missingness is detected from a group’s expected size, so the decoder does not need per-source timeout queues. It also does not store the actual path of any token.

C. Computational and Communication Costs

Proposition 1 (Implementation cost). In the word-RAM model with indexed source identifiers, graph preprocessing takes O(n+|E|)O(n+|E|) time and O(n)O(n) auxiliary space, excluding storage of the input graph. Proof. Breadth-first search visits every vertex and adjacency entry a constant number of times [8]. On a ring lattice, the maps can be calculated from vertex indices, so the general O(k)O(k) static map is unnecessary. The dynamic state remains proportional to the sink degree. The optional computation of a risk certificate in Section IV is distinct from computing the decision. Group sizes may be sorted once in O(qlogq)O(q\log q) time, after which the required largest-group sums can be evaluated in an O(q)O(q) pass.

WPEF adds no routing round, path header, or retransmission to the specified protocol. Arrival rounds are measured locally. This does not make total communication linear in kk: a loose bound for naive persistent forwarding is O(T|E|n)O(T|E|n) token-edge transmissions before batching. All detectors use that same delivery process. Topology discovery, signature verification, and source-buffer storage are excluded from Proposition 1 and must not be counted as eliminated overhead.

B. Interpretation and Scope of the Certificate

Theorem 1 uses a graph-level implication to consume part of the attack budget. It does not assign a posterior probability of honesty to each source. The certificate is conservative in two ways: an erased group can contain loyal nodes, and every retained corrupted group is treated as potentially contributing only wrong votes. A large group can therefore make the bound in (14) uninformative. The theorem states validity, not uniform tightness.

The observable quantity K𝒥K_{\mathcal J} measures the evidence retained after erasure, whereas Λ𝒥\Lambda_{\mathcal J} bounds the part that still requires adversarial treatment. Two networks with the same sink degree can have very different group-size distributions and consequently different bounds. This is why extension to arbitrary known graphs does not imply topology-independent effectiveness.

Averaging (14) over erased sets gives an unconditional upper bound for each fixed action. Computing the corresponding worst-case average still requires the action-dependent distribution of flags. Alternatively, the maximum of its right-hand side over admissible erased sets is a distribution-free but generally more conservative upper bound. Neither operation is required for the online decision.

VII. Discussion

A. Resilience, Not Universal Message Recovery

The certificate establishes that a flagged witness group contains at least one compromised node. It does not establish that all preserved messages are authentic measurements or that every attacker is detected. Authentic source labels can still be incorrect because sensing is noisy. Even when every attacked group is removed, the remaining error is the honest-majority error, not zero.

The trusted sink also changes the meaning of resilience relative to a consensus-threshold formulation. A local-only detector always retains one noisy but uncompromised observation. The question is therefore whether a defense maintains network information value while limiting worst-case error, not whether an attacker can eliminate all possibility of a correct decision. The clean budget in (6) makes that distinction operational.

B. Conservatism and Dependence on the Witness Partition

Large root branches make erasure expensive. A different fixed shortest-path tree can change branch sizes without changing the graph or flooding process. The current algorithm uses a predetermined construction and does not optimize over trees. Its guarantee applies to the selected partition; it is not a statement that BFS tie-breaking is performance optimal.

Likewise, the residue-based ring grouping should not be replaced silently by an arbitrary BFS output during reproduction. Both are valid witness partitions, but their risks may differ. A future partition-design method would be a new algorithmic component with its own computational and statistical costs.

C. Limits of Timing Certification

The soundness implication in (15) depends on deterministic normal delivery. Random loss, queueing, clock errors, and untrusted topology can flag a fully loyal group. A tolerance or stochastic delivery model might address these effects, but it would change the certificate and require a new analysis. The present results do not justify adding a timing threshold while retaining the same theorem.

Full is defined here by exact first-arrival records, although WPEF only needs an on-time/late distinction. The theory does not say that more precise timestamps always improve the implemented rule. It separates observation redundancy, additional structural certificates, and strict decision improvement; these are different claims.

D. Scope of Minimax and of the Evidence

Corollary 2 proves constrained pure minimax attainment in one explicit family. Theorem 2 gives exact WPEF risk on a larger unique-route family but does not identify its global minimax detector. Theorem 1 is broader in topology, but its probability bound depends on the specified noninteracting attack semantics. None of these results establishes a guarantee against unrestricted Byzantine behavior, source impersonation, arbitrary relay rewriting, or mobile compromised sets.

The independent checks support the equations under those assumptions. They do not replace a matched benchmark or establish novelty against every prior publication. In particular, the contribution should be distinguished from audit-bit and trust-assisted defenses [5], [6] through the network-derived certificate, conditional-risk analysis, and information-class separation, rather than by claiming that metadata-based filtering has not been studied.

VIII. Conclusion

We studied finite-horizon binary sensing at a trusted sink under source falsification and selective relay censoring. WPEF uses shortest-path witness groups to turn missing or late evidence into a group-level attack certificate, then erases flagged groups before majority fusion. The analysis yields a residual-budget guarantee and a conditional risk bound that does not assume all retained observations are unbiased. Unique deadline routes permit an exact single-attacker formula, while a two-round ring admits pure minimax attainment and a strict Count–ID separation under a common clean-performance constraint. These results connect the information hierarchy to an executable detector rather than only to nested decision classes. The algorithm retains linear online processing without attack-distribution training. Completing matched simulator and broader network validation is necessary before drawing general performance conclusions.
